A single misaligned box can ruin a slide faster than a typo in the CEO’s speech. And yet, almost every deck I open has at least one of these five alignment traps. The good news? You don’t need a designer’s eye or a day of fiddling. Fix all five in one click. Here’s how.

## The “I’ll eyeball it” disaster

You drag a shape 2 cm left. It looks right on screen. On the projector, it’s floating. The culprit? PowerPoint’s grid snaps to 0.13 cm, but your eye works in pixels. A shape that looks flush on your 4K monitor can drift by half a centimetre on a conference room screen.

So grab the ruler view. Go to View → Ruler. Every object now snaps to exact measurements. No more guessing. If you’re in a hurry, hold Alt while dragging. It overrides the grid and lets you place objects freely without affecting snaps.

## Centred text that isn’t

You centre a headline. It looks centred. Then you add an icon on the left. Suddenly the text shifts right. Centring is relative to the whole slide, not the visible content. That’s why your slide title creeps off-centre every time you insert a logo.

Fix it by grouping the text and the icon first. Select both, then centre. Now the centre is relative to the group, not the slide. If you do this often, create a text-box template with the icon already anchored. Drop it on every slide. Problem solved.

## Baseline drift on bullet lists

You copy a bullet list from another slide. The spacing between lines changes. The baseline of the text drifts up or down. PowerPoint keeps the paragraph spacing from the source slide, not the destination master.

To reset it, right-click the text box → Paragraph → set “Before” and “After” spacing to 0pt. Then adjust the line spacing to 1.0 or 1.15. Do this once, save it as a Quick Style, and reuse it forever. Your lists will stay locked to the baseline grid.

## Shapes that refuse to align with the master

You drop a new shape onto a slide. It ignores the master’s grid. You nudge it into place. Three slides later, the same shape is 3 mm left of the master’s guide. PowerPoint lets masters define background objects, but foreground shapes override them.

To force alignment, select the shape → Format → Align → Snap to Grid. Now it respects the master’s grid. If the grid isn’t visible, go to View → Gridlines. Turn it on temporarily, align, then turn it off. The shape stays put even when the grid disappears.

## Slide-to-slide spacing chaos

You copy a slide. The spacing between elements changes slightly. The gap between two placeholders widens by 0.2 cm on the new slide. PowerPoint doesn’t copy relative spacing; it copies absolute positions. So if your master has a 1 cm gap, copying a slide with a 1 cm gap won’t preserve that gap if the elements moved.

To fix it, use Slide Sorter view. Select the slides you copied. Drag the placeholders until the spacing matches the master. If you do this often, create a “spacing template” slide. Drop it before your real slide. Copy and paste it whenever you duplicate slides. The spacing stays consistent.
