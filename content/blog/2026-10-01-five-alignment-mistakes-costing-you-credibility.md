Your slide looks fine until someone sits three metres away from the screen. Then the misaligned text box screams at them. The shape that's just slightly off-centre creates visual tension. The inconsistent spacing between elements makes everything feel sloppy.

These aren't edge cases. Alignment mistakes are the number one reason otherwise solid decks look unprofessional. And here's the thing: you can fix almost all of them in seconds once you know what to look for.

## 1. Eyeballing centering instead of using guides

You drag a text box across your slide, squint at it, and think it looks centred. It's not. Your eye is a terrible instrument for alignment. It's biased by surrounding elements and optical illusions.

PowerPoint has built-in guides. Most people never touch them. Go to View and turn on Guides. Now when you drag an object toward the centre, you'll see a vertical line snap into place. That line is the exact centre of your slide. Drag anything to it. Done.

Better yet, select multiple objects and use the alignment tools in the Home ribbon. Click Align > Align Center. One click. Everything lines up perfectly. You stop guessing.

## 2. Mixing left, centre, and right alignment in the same deck

You centre headlines on one slide, left-align them on the next, then centre again on slide seven. Your audience doesn't notice consciously. But they feel the chaos.

Pick one alignment system and stick with it across every slide. If you're centring your titles, centre all your titles. If you're left-aligning body text, left-align it everywhere. This isn't about rules. It's about training your audience's eye so they can focus on your message, not on figuring out where to look.

Check your [brand guidelines](https://tlbr.io/#features) if you have them. They should dictate this. If you don't have guidelines yet, make a decision now and document it. You'll thank yourself when you build your next deck.

## 3. Leaving inconsistent white space between elements

A shape is 10 pixels from the edge of the slide. Another shape is 15 pixels away. Text sits 20 pixels from the top. It all looks random, and that randomness signals carelessness.

Use consistent spacing throughout. If your margin is 20 pixels on the left, make it 20 pixels everywhere. If you space list items 12 pixels apart, maintain that gap on every list.

PowerPoint has a feature most people miss. Select multiple objects, then go to Arrange > Distribute. Choose either Distribute Horizontally or Distribute Vertically. PowerPoint calculates the gaps and spaces them equally. One click and all that guesswork vanishes.

## 4. Centring every single element on the slide

Some designers centre headlines, centre bodies, centre images, centre everything. The result feels static. Lifeless.

Left-align most of your content. Your eye naturally reads left-to-right. When you align elements to the left, they feel active and intentional. Save centring for moments you want to emphasise: a single headline, a key statistic, a bold image.

This isn't a hard rule. But asymmetrical layouts feel more dynamic. They give you rhythm. Right now, scan any modern presentation design resource and count how many decks use strict centring. Not many.

## 5. Stacking text boxes instead of using proper formatting

You create one text box for your headline, another for your subtitle, another for your body copy. Each one drifts slightly different. The baselines don't align. The spacing between them looks uneven.

Use a single text box with proper paragraph formatting instead. Build your hierarchy with font size, weight, and colour. PowerPoint handles the spacing automatically. Your baselines align. Everything sits on an invisible grid.

If you're managing dozens of decks across teams, this gets harder to enforce manually. [Templated slides with locked formatting](https://tlbr.io/blog) solve this problem. Everyone works within a system. No drift. No guesswork.

## Fix it today

Open your current deck. Find one slide with misaligned elements. Turn on Guides. Use the Align tools. Watch how instantly better it looks.

Then do the same for your next three slides. You'll develop the habit. Your slides will look sharper. Your audience will focus on what you're saying instead of wondering why things look slightly off.

Alignment isn't flashy. It's invisible when done right. That's exactly why it matters.
