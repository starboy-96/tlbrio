Your sales deck just cost you a seven-figure deal. Not because your numbers were wrong or your case studies weak, but because the client’s eyes glazed over slide three and never came back. That’s what happens when your deck does more harm than good.

## The deck is talking, but no one’s listening

You’ve spent weeks refining your pitch. Every slide is packed with data, bullet points, and tiny font because you wanted to cover all bases. The problem? No one reads dense slides. Studies show audiences retain just 10% of what they see on a cluttered slide.

Worse, your deck screams “corporate template” from slide one. It’s the same layout as every other vendor pitching this week. The client’s brain registers it as noise, not signal. Your deck isn’t selling your solution. It’s selling mediocrity.

## Brand consistency isn’t optional—it’s a trust signal

Your logo is in the wrong position. The colours look like they were pulled from a style guide from 2018. The client’s legal team just noticed your font doesn’t match their brand guidelines. Each inconsistency chips away at their confidence.

Brand consistency isn’t about vanity. It’s about showing you pay attention to details. When your deck looks like it belongs to your company, it signals professionalism. When it looks like it belongs anywhere, it signals carelessness.

Fix this by creating a single, branded master deck. Use it as a template for every pitch. Every slide, every chart, every word should feel like it comes from the same place. If you’re constantly tweaking colours and fonts, you’re wasting time that should go into refining your message.

## The first five slides decide if you stay in the room

Slide one: a stock photo of a handshake. Slide two: your company history since 1998. Slide three: a logo salad of every client you’ve ever had. By slide five, the client’s already checked their phone.

Your opening slides need to do one thing: make the client lean in. Start with their problem, not your product. Use a bold statement, a striking visual, or a surprising stat. Then, immediately show how you solve it.

Most sales decks are selfish. They focus on the seller’s story, not the buyer’s journey. Flip that. Start with the client’s pain points. Make them feel understood before you ever mention your solution.

## Death by bullet points and tiny text

Your slide on market size has 14 bullet points in 8-point font. Your competitor analysis slide is a spreadsheet you copied and pasted. Your client stares at this for six seconds before giving up.

Slides are visual aids, not documents. If you need bullet points, keep them to five or fewer. Use a font size no smaller than 24 points. If you can’t read it from the back of the room, it’s too small.

Replace dense text with simple visuals. A chart, a diagram, or even a well-placed icon can convey your point faster than a paragraph. If you must include data, make it scannable. Use tables sparingly. Charts are better.

## The client’s brand is MIA

Every slide looks like it belongs to your company. The client’s logo only appears on the title slide, if at all. That’s a missed opportunity to show you’ve done your homework.

Embed the client’s brand into your deck subtly but consistently. Use their colours for accents. Reference their industry challenges directly. Mention their competitors in your analysis. This shows you’re not just selling a product. You’re selling a solution tailored to them.

If you’re using a generic deck for every pitch, you’re missing a chance to build rapport. Tailor at least the first third of your deck to the client’s world. It doesn’t take long, but it makes a huge difference.

## The last slide is where deals go to die

Your closing slide is a contact form. Or worse, a blank slide with “Questions?” in the middle. The client sits in silence, unsure of what to do next. They leave the meeting without a next step.

Your deck should guide the client to a decision. End with a clear call to action. It could be a proposal, a next meeting, or a timeline for next steps. Make it easy for them to say yes.

Include a slide that recaps the key takeaways. Highlight the problem, your solution, and the value you bring. Then, invite the client to engage. Ask a question. Offer a next step. Silence is not your friend.

## Fix it today

Open your sales deck right now. Ask yourself:

- Does it start with the client’s problem or my product?
- Is every slide scannable in under 10 seconds?
- Does it look and feel like it belongs to my company?
- Does it end with a clear next step?

If the answer to any of these is no, you’re losing deals before you speak. Take two hours today to strip out the noise. Make it visual. Make it client-focused. Make it yours.

Your next deal depends on it.
