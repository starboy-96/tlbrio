A sales deck has a strange job. It has to stand in for you when you are presenting, and again when you are not in the room at all, forwarded to a finance director who never heard your pitch. In both cases it is quietly shaping the buyer's view of how carefully you work.

Most deals are not lost on a single slide. They slip away through small signals that add up: a cluttered page, an opening that talks only about the seller, an ending with no clear next step. Here are the patterns most worth checking, along with what to do instead.

1. **It opens with you, not them.** A company history, an office map and a wall of client logos tell the buyer what you think matters. Open with their problem in their language, and earn the right to talk about yourself later.

2. **The slides are documents in disguise.** Fourteen bullet points in small type will not be read in a meeting, and barely read afterwards. Keep one idea per slide, use a title that states the point, and move the detail into an appendix or a leave-behind.

3. **Data arrives raw.** A table pasted from a spreadsheet asks the buyer to do the analysis for you. Turn it into a simple chart, highlight the one number that matters, and say in the title what the reader should conclude.

4. **The deck looks stitched together.** Different fonts, three shades of the brand colour and a logo in a slightly different place on every page all suggest a team that does not pay attention to detail. Building from one branded master, with colours and fonts available in a click rather than chosen by eye, removes most of this without extra effort from the sales team.

5. **It could have been sent to anyone.** If nothing in the first few slides refers to the buyer's sector, competitors or stated priorities, they will assume it is the same deck everyone gets. Tailor the opening third, even if the rest stays standard.

6. **The ending asks nothing.** A final slide reading "Questions?" leaves the buyer to decide what happens next, and often nothing does. Close with a short recap of the problem and your answer, then propose a specific next step with a date attached.

7. **Nobody has read it cold.** The people who built the deck know what every slide is meant to say, so they cannot see where it is unclear. Hand it to a colleague who was not involved and ask them to explain the pitch back to you after a single pass.

None of these fixes requires a redesign. Most can be done in an afternoon with the deck you already have. Pick your most recent live opportunity, run its deck against the seven points above, and fix the two that apply most clearly before your next meeting.
