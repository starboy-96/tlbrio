Your meeting notes are forty slides long. Somewhere between slide 28 and the conclusion, someone asks: "What was the actual decision we made?"

This is becoming a recognisable problem. As AI presentation tools make it trivial to turn documents into slides—generating bullet points, speaker notes and visual layouts automatically—teams are discovering that more slides rarely clarify thinking. Often the opposite.

The mechanics are seductive. Feed a report, research document or meeting transcript into an AI tool, and within seconds you have a structured deck: one slide per section, each populated with extracted summaries. No manual layout. No decisions about what actually matters. The tool has done the thinking for you.

But here's what's changed. When slides took effort to create, scarcity enforced discipline. You chose what was worth presenting. You discarded the supporting detail. The audience saw your reasoning, not your source material reorganised.

Automated slides invert this. Because generation is free and fast, the temptation is to include everything—every data point, every quote, every context that might be relevant. The tool makes this effortless. The human cost is invisible until the meeting room.

The problem compounds in remote settings. On a video call, a 40-slide deck is a performance tax on everyone. Attendees cannot follow dense slides while listening. Presenters read text aloud. Time that should go to decision-making goes to information transfer that could have happened asynchronously in a document.

This matters beyond individual meetings. Teams using AI to generate slides are often the same teams that haven't yet established what decision needs making, or who needs to make it. The slides become a substitute for clarity, not an expression of it.

The useful question is not "Can AI create slides from this?" but "Does this conversation need a slide deck at all, or would this be clearer as a document, a recorded summary, or a focussed five-slide narrative?"

If you do need slides: constrain the AI output. Use it to draft speaker notes or to structure thinking—not to define what the audience sees. Then edit ruthlessly. Cut every slide that doesn't move the decision forward. Check that text is readable from the back of the room. Let the slides support the conversation, not replace it.

The best presentation tool you have is still your own judgment about what matters.
