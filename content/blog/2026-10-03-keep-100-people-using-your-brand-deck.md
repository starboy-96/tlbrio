Your brand guidelines document sits in a Google Drive folder. It looks sharp. Nobody reads it.

By the time you hit 100 people, you have 100 different interpretations of your brand in PowerPoint. Someone in sales uses the old logo. Marketing has invented three new colour variations. Finance is using Comic Sans. Not quite, but you get the point.

The problem isn't that people want to break your brand standards. It's that maintaining brand consistency across a large distributed team feels impossible when you're using vanilla PowerPoint. People forget which font to use. They grab the wrong blue. They slot their own images in without checking the guidelines. And then they send decks to clients and prospects looking like they came from five different companies.

## Start with a master deck, not just a guide

A written brand guide is necessary. It's not sufficient.

You need a master PowerPoint file that people actually use as their starting point. This file should have your approved fonts, colour palette, margins, and slide layouts built in. When someone opens it, they're already working within your brand boundaries. They can't accidentally use the wrong shade of blue because it's not available.

Build five to eight core slide layouts. That's it. Not fifty. Title slides, content slides with images, comparison slides, data slides, section dividers. Use your master slide feature so when you change the font across all 100 decks, it updates automatically.

The magic happens when your team treats this master deck as their template. They duplicate it. They start working. They stay on brand without thinking about it.

## Make it stupidly easy to find and use

Your master deck needs to live somewhere everyone knows about and can access instantly.

If people have to hunt through three folders and email someone to get the latest version, they won't. They'll use whatever's on their desktop or, worse, start from scratch. Put it in a shared drive with a clear filename. Version it clearly. When you update it, tell your team. Really tell them. Not a Slack message buried in a channel. Send an email. Make the update feel real.

## Assign one person to own this

You need a brand owner. Someone whose job includes maintaining PowerPoint standards.

This doesn't mean they make every deck. It means they maintain the master file, monitor that slides are on-brand, train people when they join, and update the system when your brand evolves. They should have clear authority to say no to off-brand decks before they get shared externally.

Give this person access to a central asset library. They need to know which logos are current, which images align with your brand, which fonts you've licensed. When someone asks "can I use this image?", they should be the arbiter.

## Train people once, then again, then once more

You'll onboard new people constantly. Each person needs to know the standards.

Make the training real. Not a 40-minute video nobody watches. Show them the master deck. Walk through it. Tell them why these choices matter (not just that they do). Show them common mistakes. Give them a checklist to use before they send a deck anywhere.

Even long-serving people forget. Revisit brand standards once a quarter. Send a short memo. Share a before-and-after example of a deck that was fixed. Make it visual so people see the difference consistency makes.

## Audit and give feedback

You can't assume people will stick to brand standards just because they know about them.

Look at decks before they go out. Really look. Catch the things that break. Don't be punitive. Just fix them, send them back with a note, and help people understand why consistency matters to your brand. Most people respond well when they understand the reasoning.
