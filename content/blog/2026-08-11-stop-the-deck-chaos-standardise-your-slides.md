Most organisations treat inconsistent slides as a cosmetic irritation. I think that undersells the problem. When every team builds decks its own way, the cost shows up in places nobody budgets for: reviews that drag on over formatting, executives who cannot find the slide they saw last month, and clients who notice that three documents from the same firm look like they came from three different firms.

Standardising presentations is usually framed as a trade-off between control and creativity. That framing is the reason so many attempts fail. People hear "standard" and imagine sterile, identical decks, so they resist, and the standards team responds by tightening the rules, and the resistance grows.

The useful question is narrower. Which parts of a slide carry your brand, and which parts carry the thinking? Colours, fonts, logo placement, spacing and the look of a chart belong to the first group. Nobody's argument improves because they picked a slightly different blue. The structure of the story, the choice of evidence and the emphasis belong to the second group, and that is where people should spend their energy.

Seen this way, standardisation takes decisions off people's plates. A consultant who no longer has to think about fonts or footer positions has more attention for the recommendation on slide six. A shared template means a slide from one deck can move to another without being rebuilt.

## Standards that live in a PDF will lose

The weakest point in most programmes is location. The rules live in a brand guide, the template lives in a folder, and the work happens in PowerPoint, often at speed and late in the day. Under pressure, people grab whatever is closest, which is usually last quarter's deck.

If standards are to hold, they need to sit inside the tool where slides are made. Approved colours and fonts should be one click away. Templates and approved layouts should be in the ribbon, not three folders deep. Tidy alignment should be quicker than dragging boxes by eye. When following the rules is the fastest route, most people follow them without being asked.

That is also why enforcement by audit and reminder email tends to disappoint. It arrives after the deck is finished, when fixing it feels like punishment. Guidance that arrives while the slide is being built feels like help.

The organisations that get this right are rarely the strictest. They are the ones that made the standard the easiest path and then got out of the way. If you want to test your own setup, try this: ask someone outside the brand team to build a short deck from scratch without opening the style guide, then look at what comes back. Whatever they got wrong is where your system needs work.
