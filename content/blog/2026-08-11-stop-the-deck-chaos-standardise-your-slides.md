# Stop the deck chaos — standardise your slides

Your last quarterly review deck had 17 fonts, 11 brand colours that don’t match the style guide, and a logo stretched to breaking point. Sound familiar?

When 50 colleagues build their own slides, brand drift isn’t a bug, it’s inevitable. The result: executive decks that feel like Frankenstein’s monster, built from last year’s pitch, yesterday’s webinar, and a template someone dug out of SharePoint in 2019.

Standardising presentations across a large organisation isn’t about removing creativity. It’s about protecting clarity, consistency, and credibility. Here’s how to stop the slide chaos without strangling innovation.

## Start with one source of truth

Every deck should begin with a single, locked template. Not a folder of “good examples” or a shared drive of “latest versions that may or may not be current.” One file. One place. One version.

Create a master template in PowerPoint that includes:
- Approved master slides with correct layouts
- Pre-loaded brand fonts and colours
- Auto-updating logo blocks
- Slide numbers and footer text

Lock the colours so no one can “just tweak” the palette. Freeze the fonts so users can’t “experiment” with Comic Sans after midnight. Publish the template in a shared location with read-only permissions. This isn’t control for control’s sake. It’s preventing a slide that looks like it belongs in a primary school project.

## Automate what you can, enforce what you must

Templates are useless if people treat them like suggestions. You need enforcement baked in.

- Use PowerPoint’s built-in **Slide Master** to lock layouts and fonts.
- Deploy a simple VBA macro that checks every new slide against your brand rules before it’s saved.
- Run quarterly audits with a script that flags decks using unauthorised fonts or colours.

And for teams that still “just need to add a slide,” give them a one-click **Insert New Slide from Master** button. No copying, no pasting, no drift.

## Train the rebels — they’re your best teachers

The loudest critics of standardisation are often the most productive presenters. They resist rules until they see how much time they waste fixing inconsistencies later.

Turn them into your champions by showing how standardisation actually speeds them up. One master template means no hunting for the right slide, no reformatting graphs, no arguing with marketing about the right blue.

Run a 30-minute session: show them how to customise layouts without breaking the rules. Give them a sandbox template where they can prototype new ideas safely. Once they experience the freedom of working within guardrails, they’ll police the rules themselves.

## Make compliance effortless, not onerous

Compliance isn’t about policing. It’s about removing friction from the right process.

- Embed brand guidelines into the template itself: hover text explains why the footer must stay, why the margins can’t shrink.
- Use conditional formatting to highlight slides that break rules before they’re saved.
- Provide a “Quick Fix” macro that auto-corrects the most common errors: font substitution, colour drift, logo distortion.

And when someone insists they “need” to use a non-standard font, ask them to calculate how long it takes to fix that slide in the review cycle. Usually, the realisation hits fast.

## Measure drift, celebrate alignment

You can’t fix what you don’t measure. Track inconsistency with simple metrics:
- Number of slides using off-brand fonts
- Percentage of decks with stretched logos
- Frequency of unauthorised colour usage

Publish these stats in your internal wiki or newsletter. When you see teams improving, call it out. Recognition drives behaviour faster than another email from comms.

## Embed standards in the workflow

Standards fail when they live only in a PDF on SharePoint. They must live inside PowerPoint, inside Outlook, inside Teams.

Publish your template as a company-wide default in PowerPoint Online. Add it to the “New” menu in Teams so every meeting deck starts clean. Train IT to push updates automatically so no one runs on last year’s version.

And for the love of clarity, disable the “Design Ideas” pane for brand-critical decks. It’s not helpful. It’s a chaotic suggestion engine disguised as AI.

## One thing you can do today

Pick one deck that’s already caused confusion this quarter. Open it. Count the number of fonts. Count the number of blues that don’t match the style guide. Now open your master template and rebuild it.

That’s not a long-term project. It’s a 15-minute reality check. Do it today, before someone else copies it into next month’s board deck.

Standardisation isn’t about sterile slides. It’s about making every presentation reinforce your brand, not dilute it. Start small. Enforce ruthlessly. Watch the chaos fade.
