Your brand guidelines sit in a PDF nobody reads. Someone on your team is using a font that does not exist on brand. Another person has invented their own colour palette. And your CEO is walking into client meetings holding three different versions of your logo.

This is not a design problem. It is a distribution problem.

Maintaining brand standards across PowerPoint for 100+ people requires you to stop treating it like a once-a-year training session. You need systems that make it easier to do the right thing than the wrong thing.

Start by building a shared template library that lives somewhere your team actually uses. Not a folder buried in SharePoint. Not a PDF sent round in 2019. A real, living set of master slides that people grab when they start a new deck. Make sure these templates are locked down so people cannot accidentally delete your approved colour palette. The template should feel restrictive to someone trying to break brand. It should feel frictionless to someone trying to follow it.

Next, establish clear ownership. One person should own brand standards in PowerPoint. Not a committee. Not a design desk that reviews decks after they are finished. One person who publishes updates when something changes and communicates why.

Then audit what people are actually doing. Pull a sample of 20 decks from across your organisation. You will find violations you did not know existed. Document them. Do not shame anyone. Instead, update your template or your guidance to close the gap. Most people break brand because the right option was invisible or harder than the wrong option.

Finally, use tools that enforce standards without feeling like bureaucracy. [A PowerPoint add-in can lock down fonts, enforce colour palettes, and catch brand violations before anyone shares a deck.](https://tlbr.io/#features) This is not about being the design police. It is about removing the friction between good intentions and good execution.

Your brand does not decay because people do not care. It decays because caring takes effort. Make consistency the path of least resistance, and your standards will hold at scale.
