When you look through a designer's portfolio now, how do you know whose ability you are looking at?

It is a fair question, and an uncomfortable one for anyone hiring. Image generators, layout suggestions and generative fill in mainstream design tools mean that someone who could not have made a finished-looking piece a few years ago can now produce one. That is not a scandal. Designers should use the tools available to them. But it does break something hiring managers have relied on for a long time.

Polish was always a proxy. A beautifully finished case study suggested hours of craft. Craft suggested someone who understood type, hierarchy, colour and the hundred small decisions behind a page. The proxy worked because finish was expensive. As finish gets cheaper, it says less about the person who produced it.

> When finish becomes cheap, the ideas a designer rejected become the best evidence of how they think.

## Ask to see the decisions

What remains scarce is judgement. That means reading a vague brief and working out the real problem, choosing between three plausible directions, knowing which constraint to push against and which to respect, and explaining all of that to a sceptical client. None of it shows up reliably in a final image. All of it shows up in the decisions that led there.

Here is one practical change. When you invite a candidate to interview, ask them to bring one project with the original brief, two directions they abandoned and a sentence on why each was dropped. A strong designer will find this easy because they remember the argument. Someone relying on surface finish will find it harder because there may not have been an argument.

A second test is even simpler. Give the candidate a specific audience and a competent but generic piece of work, the kind a tool produces in seconds. Ask what they would change and why. Fluency here is hard to fake. It reveals whether they can see the gap between acceptable and right.

None of this means craft no longer matters. Someone still has to set the type properly, build files a developer or printer can use, and spot the detail a generator got subtly wrong. But craft is now one thing to check, not the thing that stands in for everything else.

This next part is a prediction, not an established trend. Strong portfolios will probably start to look less like galleries and more like case files, with fewer hero images and more briefs, sketches, dead ends and reasoning. Designers who already document their thinking have an advantage. Hiring teams that still scan for the prettiest grid in thirty seconds may be measuring the tools instead of the people.
