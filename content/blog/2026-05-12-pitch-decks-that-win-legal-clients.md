# Pitch decks that win legal clients

Your last pitch deck didn’t get a reply. Not because your firm lacks expertise, but because your slides read like a tax manual in Comic Sans. Lawyers and consultants wince at decks that bury insights under walls of text. Here’s how to fix that.

## Start with the client’s pain, not your pedigree

Stop listing qualifications on slide one. The GC who hired you last quarter didn’t care about your rankings; they cared that you reduced their contract cycle time by three weeks.

Put their problem up front.
- A clear statement of their challenge in their own words
- A metric they already track (cost per matter, time to close, regulatory risk)
- A one-line teaser of your solution

Slide one should feel like reading their in-house memo, not your firm brochure.

## Use fewer words, bigger meaning

I once saw a partner cram 47 bullet points onto a single slide. The client’s eyes glazed over before slide two.

Rule of thumb: if you can read the slide silently in under eight seconds, you’re on the right track. Anything longer and your audience starts translating text into their own words—while you’re still talking.

Try this instead:
- Replace paragraphs with a single sentence, then add the detail in your spoken commentary.
- Use icons before each point (document, clock, gavel) so the eye can scan instantly.
- Limit body text to 30 words per slide. If you need more, split the content across two slides.

## Visuals that look like legal documents—only sharper

Legal decks often mimic client contracts: dense, muted colours, footnotes in 8-point font. That’s the fastest way to make a GC reach for their phone.

Use visuals that feel premium without feeling corporate.
- High-quality headshots of the team (no passport photos).
- Clean timelines with only the key milestones highlighted.
- Process flowcharts in a single accent colour, not rainbow chaos.
- White space around every element so it breathes.

If your slide could be mistaken for a Terms & Conditions page, redesign it.

## Slide design that travels as well as your team

A partner once emailed his deck to a client at 9 p.m. on a Friday. The client opened it on an iPhone and squinted for five minutes before giving up.

PowerPoint isn’t just for boardrooms anymore. Your deck must work on a 6-inch screen.

Checklist for mobile-ready slides:
- Text minimum 24 pt, headings 36 pt.
- Contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for readability.
- Avoid graphics wider than 800 px so they don’t scroll sideways.
- Test colours on a grayscale printout—if it’s not readable, change it.

## Tell the client what to do tomorrow

Great pitches end with a clear next step. Too many decks say “We’d welcome the opportunity to discuss further” and leave the client scrolling for the contact page.

Make the call to action unmissable.
- Add a slide titled “Next steps” with three bullet points in bold.
- Include a calendar link that lets the client book a meeting while the deck is still open.
- Put the partner’s direct email and mobile on the last slide—no gatekeepers.

If your deck doesn’t tell the client exactly how to say yes, you’ve already said no for them.

## One thing you can do today

Open your latest pitch deck. Count the words on the first slide. If there are more than ten, delete half and replace the rest with a client pain statement. Then check every slide on your phone. If it doesn’t look sharp in portrait mode, spend 15 minutes fixing the font size and contrast. Ship it to yourself and forward it to a colleague for a second opinion. Close more deals tomorrow by fixing your deck today.
