Picture a design team at a mid-sized consultancy, late on a Thursday. A partner forwards a 30-slide client deck with a short note: "Can you make this look right before 9am?" Nobody on the design team wrote a word of it. They open the file and start the familiar sweep.

Slide four uses a font nobody has installed. Slide nine has a logo stretched sideways to fill a gap. The charts on slides twelve to fifteen were pasted from three different spreadsheets, each in its own default colours. Two designers spend the evening nudging boxes, re-typing headings and rebuilding charts. The deck goes out on time. Next week, a different partner sends a different deck, and the same evening repeats.

The people who built those slides did nothing unusual. They are analysts and consultants working quickly in a program that lets them choose any font, any colour and any layout. Without guardrails at the point of creation, the clean-up simply moves downstream to the only people who know what the brand should look like.

## What changes when the fix moves upstream

- **Brand colours and fonts sit in the toolbar.** The author picks from the approved set instead of whatever PowerPoint offers by default, so there is less to correct later.
- **Alignment takes one click.** Boxes that would have been dragged roughly into place are aligned and distributed properly the first time.
- **Charts and tables start formatted.** Pasted data can be brought into house style without a designer rebuilding it by hand.
- **Designers get their evenings back.** Their time goes on new templates, campaign work and the slides that genuinely need a designer's eye.

None of this turns a finance analyst into a designer, and it does not need to. The aim is a first draft that already follows the rules, so the design team reviews rather than rescues. If your designers are the last line of defence for every deck, start by counting how many of their hours last month went on fixing slides they did not create. That number tells you where the problem actually sits.
