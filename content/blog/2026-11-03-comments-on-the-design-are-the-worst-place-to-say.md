Most creative teams assume the clearest feedback is feedback placed directly on the work. Click the headline, type the note, tag the designer. Most design, video and document tools now make this easy, and it feels precise.

It is precise. That is the problem.

A comment pinned to a headline invites a change to that headline. It rarely invites the sentence a project sometimes needs: "this whole page is answering the wrong question." Structural problems don't sit at a coordinate, so they tend not to get said. Or they get said as twelve small notes that each treat a symptom. It's easy to reach round three having fixed forty details on a concept nobody was sure about.

The answer isn't to abandon comments. It's to recognise that the main ways of collecting feedback do different jobs, and to choose deliberately. Here is how three common approaches compare.

| | Comments pinned to the file | One consolidated feedback note | Live review session |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strongest at | Precise, local fixes | Setting priorities and settling disagreements | Judging direction, story and overall feel |
| Tends to miss | Structural and strategic problems | Exact placement of small details | Detail, and anything nobody writes down |
| Conflicting opinions | Passed to the designer to referee | Resolved before the designer sees them | Resolved in the room, if the decision-maker attends |
| Record for later | Strong, tied to the exact element | Strong, if filed with the version reviewed | Weak without a written summary |
| Hidden cost | Many voices, no owner | One person's time to merge and decide | Everyone's diary at once |
| Best stage | Final polish | Middle rounds | First look at concepts |

The most telling row is the third. When five stakeholders comment directly on a file, contradictions arrive unfiltered. The marketing lead wants the logo bigger, and the brand manager wants more space around it. The designer becomes the referee without the authority to make the call. The volume of comments also starts to look like progress, which makes it harder to notice that nobody has said whether the work is right.

The consolidated note can look like admin, a step that slows things down. But the work it represents has to happen somewhere. Someone has to decide which feedback matters and which note wins when two clash. If the client lead or account manager doesn't do this in a note, the designer does it by guesswork, often after making both changes to see which survives. If you use an AI assistant to tidy a long thread, keep the distinction clear: a summary tells you what was said, not what was decided.

Live review is the only one of the three where tone and intent come through, which is why it suits early concepts. Its weakness is memory. Two people can leave the same call with different versions of what was agreed. A five-line written summary sent the same day, listing decisions rather than discussion, turns the most useful conversation into the most reliable record.

## Bottom line: match the method to the round

Before each review, state in the request what kind of feedback you want: direction, priorities or polish. Then use the channel built for it. Hold a live session for direction, write a consolidated note for priorities and use pinned comments for polish.

To check your current process, look back at your last project and tag each pinned comment as either detail or structure. If structural comments are still appearing in the final rounds, the direction conversation probably never happened properly. It was spread across the file as small notes, and nobody had to own it.
