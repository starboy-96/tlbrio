Law firms put enormous care into the substance of a pitch and comparatively little into the slides that carry it. That gap is understandable. Partners are trained to be thorough, and thoroughness on a slide tends to look like dense text. But a prospective client often meets the deck before they meet the team, sometimes on a phone between meetings. The questions below come up again and again when firms try to improve their pitch materials.

### Doesn't a serious client expect detail?

They expect rigour, which is different. Detail belongs in the proposal document, the engagement letter and the conversation. The deck's job is to show that you understand the client's situation and have a clear way through it.

A useful test is to read each slide title on its own. If the titles alone tell the story, the deck is doing its job. If they read "Overview", "Our Experience" and "Approach", the client has to dig for the point.

### Should we open with our rankings and history?

Most firms do, which is exactly why it makes little impression. Every competitor in the room will have offices, awards and a long track record. Opening with the client's problem, stated plainly and specifically, shows you have listened before you start selling.

Your credentials still matter. They simply land better once the client knows what they are evidence for.

### How many slides is too many?

There is no correct number, but every slide should earn its place. A short structure that moves from the problem, through your insight and approach, to the team, the fees and the next step will cover most pitches. Supporting material can go in an appendix.

If you find yourself adding slides to show how much the firm has done, that is usually the moment to cut. A tightly edited deck also suggests a team that will be disciplined with the client's matter.

### Does brand consistency really matter to a client?

It matters more than most partners assume, though clients rarely say so. A deck in which fonts change, colours shift and the logo moves around reads as assembled in a hurry by several people, which is often exactly what happened. That impression sits uncomfortably next to a pitch about precision.

The practical answer is to make consistency automatic. One template, with brand colours, fonts and approved layouts built into PowerPoint, means associates assembling slides at speed still produce something that looks like one firm. ### What about the numbers?

Fees, timelines and outcomes deserve visual treatment rather than dense tables. Round figures sensibly, show the one number that matters most in your brand colour, and use a simple chart where a comparison needs to be made. If a client cannot grasp the point of a data slide almost at once, simplify it.

### What is the quickest improvement we could make?

Take your most recent pitch deck and give it to a colleague who is not a lawyer. Ask them to explain, in a couple of sentences, what problem you were solving and why the client should choose you. Wherever they hesitate is where the deck needs work, and that is where to start before the next pitch.
