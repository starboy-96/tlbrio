You've got 100 people making presentations. By next week, you'll have 100 different colour palettes, font sizes, and ways of structuring a slide. Someone in marketing will use Calibri. Someone in sales will build a deck that looks like it was designed in 2007. And your brand will look fractured and chaotic.

Maintaining brand standards across a large team in PowerPoint isn't a design problem. It's a process problem. And most teams get it wrong because they treat it like both.

## The real problem with brand guidelines

You probably have a brand guide somewhere. A beautiful PDF that nobody reads. Or a Figma file that lives on a shared drive that half your team doesn't know exists.

The guide sits there, 40 pages of dos and don'ts, and then people open PowerPoint and immediately default to whatever they remember or whatever feels easiest. Your guidelines become a theoretical exercise, not a practical tool.

Here's what actually works: make the standards invisible.

## Build a template, not a straitjacket

A proper template isn't a blank slide with your logo in the corner. It's a complete system that people can't break even if they try.

Your template should lock down the things that matter. Colours. Typography. Spacing. Imagery style. Heading hierarchy. Then leave room for people to think.

If you give someone a slide master with three pre-built layouts (title, content with sidebar, full-bleed image), they can't accidentally use Comic Sans. They can't pick a random shade of blue. The system makes compliance effortless.

Make your template beautiful to use, though. If it's clunky or limited, people will work around it. They'll copy text into a blank slide instead. They'll start from scratch just to get more flexibility.

Test your template with someone who's never used it before. If they struggle or feel restricted, redesign it.

## Centralise the source of truth

Your template needs to live in exactly one place. Preferably not an email attachment or an old shared drive folder.

One team we worked with had seventeen versions of their "master template" floating around. Four were from 2021. Two had conflicting colour codes. Nobody knew which one to use, so people just grabbed whatever was closest.

Pick a system. Whether that's a shared drive, a template library, or a dedicated tool, make it the only place people go. Make it searchable. Make it easy to download. Make it obvious which version is current.

Version your templates properly. If you update colours or fonts, version 2.0 should be unmistakable. Old versions should still be findable for people who need them, but the newest version should be the default everyone sees first.

## Make compliance a feature, not a burden

The moment maintaining brand standards feels like extra work, people will cut corners.

Instead, build brand enforcement into the editing experience itself. If you're using PowerPoint, [pre-built templates with locked elements](https://tlbr.io/#features) mean people can focus on content, not formatting. They can't accidentally delete your logo. They can't change your primary colour.

When someone opens your template and starts editing, the brand is already there. Maintaining standards becomes frictionless.

## Create a feedback loop for edge cases

Your template won't cover everything. Someone will need a layout that doesn't exist. Someone will need to use a non-standard colour for accessibility reasons.

Build a simple process for this. A single Slack channel or email inbox where people ask permission, not forgiveness. Review requests within 24 hours. When you approve something, add it to your template for next time.

This isn't bureaucracy. It's learning. Every approved exception should eventually become a standard option in your template.

## The accountability piece

None of this works without someone owning it. Not the entire brand team. One person who monitors template usage, tracks which versions people are using, and notices when someone's gone rogue.

This person should spend maybe four hours a month on it. They download presentations from a shared folder. They check if people are using the latest template. They follow up when they find old versions or custom formatting.

Sounds tedious. It's not. It's maintenance.

## Start here

Pick your three core brand elements (colours, fonts, logo placement). Lock those down in your template first. You can add depth later. Get your template into people's hands this week. Not perfect, just live.

Then monitor what breaks. Where do people struggle? Where do they work around the system? That's where you redesign next.

Brand consistency at scale isn't about having perfect guidelines. It's about making compliance so frictionless that breaking the rules requires more effort than following them. Build your template right, and [your team will stay on brand without you policing every slide](https://tlbr.io/blog).
